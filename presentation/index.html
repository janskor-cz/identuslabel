01 — Identity Before DIDs

The internet was never built to know who you are.

TCP/IP has four layers — Link, Internet, Transport, Application — and none of them carry a notion of a person. An IP address identifies a network interface, not a human being: it can be shared by a household, reassigned by a carrier, or spoofed outright. Every notion of "who is this" had to be bolted on afterward, one application at a time — cookies, passwords, certificates — with no common identity layer underneath any of it.

A partial fix: ZTNA

Zero Trust Network Access tries to patch this at the network edge — instead of trusting anyone inside a perimeter, every request is checked against an identity-aware policy engine. It's a real improvement over old perimeter security, but almost every ZTNA product ties that identity check to its own vendor's proprietary control plane: swap providers, and the access policy has to be rebuilt from scratch.

01 — Identity Before DIDs

Certificates were built to identify servers, not people.

X.509 — the standard behind the padlock in a browser's address bar — is the closest thing the application layer has to an identity system. It works because there are relatively few web servers, each backed by an organization that can pay a Certificate Authority and renew a certificate on schedule. It assumes a small number of well-resourced subjects and a handful of root authorities everyone agrees to trust in advance. A person is neither of those things.

Verification doesn't scale

A CA can vet a few thousand companies a year. It cannot physically verify billions of individuals the same way.

Revocation doesn't scale

Certificate revocation lists grow without bound and must be checked online, on every single verification.

All-or-nothing disclosure

A certificate hands over the whole identity. There's no way to prove just "I'm an employee" without the rest.

01 — Identity Before DIDs

Federation just moves the same problem up one layer.

"Sign in with Google" style federation (OAuth / OIDC) fixed password fatigue by letting one large identity provider vouch for you everywhere. But now that provider sees every login, and every relying service has to trust it completely.

Chokepoint

If the identity provider goes down, nobody can sign in anywhere that depends on it — not just one service.

Surveillance

The provider sees exactly which services you use and when, across every relying party at once.

Lock-in

Lose that one account, or have it suspended, and access to everything tied to it disappears with it.

02 — A Third Option

Self-sovereign identity: the person holds the proof, not a provider.

Decentralized identifiers (DIDs) and verifiable credentials (VCs) remove the single trusted middle party entirely. There is no CA that has to scale to billions of people, and no federated provider standing between the person and the service they're proving something to.

03 — Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs)

W3C Standard · Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs) v1.0

An identity you control, defined by an open standard — not a vendor.

DIDs are specified by the W3C — the same standards body behind HTML and CSS — so this isn't a proprietary format tied to one platform. A subject gets one permanent root DID, plus a fresh, unlinkable DID for every relationship it forms. The same pattern works for a person or an AI agent acting on their behalf, not only humans.

Peer DIDs can't be correlated with each other — a document service and a training portal can't compare notes to work out they're talking to the same person, unless a credential says so. Documents follow the same root-identity pattern: a document's DID is permanent and independent of storage — it never changes even if the file is re-uploaded or moved to different storage.

04 — Verifiable Credentials

A credential three parties can trust — without a private line between them.

Here, the Certification Authority typically plays the issuer and services like the Document Service play the verifier — but the mechanism is the same for any issuer/verifier pair.

flowchart TB
    I(["Issuer"]) -->|"signs & issues"| H(["Wallet"])
    H -->|"presents proof"| V(["Verifier"])
    I -.->|"publishes DID"| BC(["Public DID Registry"])
    V -.->|"resolves DID"| BC
    style BC fill:#24365c,color:#ffffff,stroke:#24365c
    style H fill:#b8863b,color:#ffffff,stroke:#b8863b

The issuer's public key isn't sent by the issuer at verification time — it's resolved from a public blockchain ledger, the same one that anchors PRISM DIDs. Anyone can read it independently; no request to the issuer's own servers is ever made, and no live line exists between verifier and issuer.

Deployment flexibility

Resolving the registry doesn't have to mean a public internet round-trip on every check. A resolver can sit at the network edge, be reached over an internal network, and — for a limited window — verify against a locally cached copy of the ledger even while fully offline.

04 — Verifiable Credentials

Proving a fact, not handing over a file.

A credential like SecurityClearanceGrant can carry many fields — name, date of birth, employer, clearance level, issue date. Presenting all of it would leak far more than a document check needs. Selective disclosure lets the holder present only the one field a specific request actually asks for; everything else is redacted before it ever leaves the wallet.

Held in the wallet

Full credential

  • Full name
  • Date of birth
  • Employer & department
  • Clearance level
  • Issue & expiry date
Selective disclosure

Presented to the Document Service

  • Redacted field
  • Redacted field
  • Redacted field
  • Clearance level: RESTRICTED
  • Redacted field

This is enabled by the credential's format (SD-JWT) — each field is individually hashed and salted when issued, so the holder chooses, presentation by presentation, exactly which disclosures to include. Here, only the clearance level is disclosed; every other field never leaves the wallet in a readable form.

04 — Verifiable Credentials

The same model behind Europe's digital identity wallet — with more privacy still possible.

This isn't a bespoke format. The SD-JWT credential approach used here, on the W3C Verifiable Credentials data model, is the same direction the EU has standardized on for the European Digital Identity (EUDI) Wallet, mandated under eIDAS 2.0. And selective disclosure isn't the end of the road — the same data model supports going further.

Disclosure methodWhat the verifier learns
Full credentialEvery field, in the clear
Selective disclosure — used todayOnly the fields requested, as raw values
Zero-knowledge proof — possible next stepOnly whether a statement is true — e.g. "clearance ≥ RESTRICTED" — never the value itself

A zero-knowledge proof lets a holder prove a statement about a field without revealing the field's actual value at all. It's a strictly stronger privacy guarantee than selective disclosure, built on the same credential model — adopting it later wouldn't require issuers or verifiers to integrate differently.

06 — Can a DID Belong to Anyone?

Can a person hold their own DID? Can an AI agent? Can a document?

Nothing in the DID specification assumes the thing behind an identifier is a human being. The W3C standard deliberately calls it a "subject" — whoever controls the matching private key controls the DID and whatever it resolves to: a small, signed DID Document listing public keys and service endpoints. That's the entire mechanism. It doesn't care whether the key lives in a person's wallet, an AI agent's runtime, or a server minting an identifier on a document's behalf.

Person

Holds a root PRISM DID plus a fresh Peer DID per relationship — the pattern from Slide 06, controlled through the IDL Wallet.

AI agent

The same DID methods apply unchanged — an autonomous agent can hold, prove, and be verified by, its own identifier with no human in the loop.

Document

A document gets its own permanent DID too — independent of file storage, so it survives being moved, re-uploaded, or re-encrypted. Covered next.

This is why the same protocol that already carries proofs of a person's clearance is about to carry a document's identity in the next section — it isn't a special case bolted on for documents, it's the same open standard doing exactly what it was designed to do.

07 — Document Access Service

No standing account. Every document is guarded at the moment it's opened.

Stateless

No session and no login persists on the server between visits — there's nothing to expire, and nothing to steal after the fact.

Verified first open

The first time a document is opened, identity and clearance are checked in full — details on the trade-off for repeat opens ahead.

End-to-end

Each document's key is delivered through a per-holder one-time key exchange — the server never sends a key in the clear.

This is a pure Zero Trust implementation — not the network-edge patch described on Slide 02, which still routes every check through one vendor's proprietary control plane. Nothing here is ever trusted implicitly: not the network path, not a session cookie, not even a document that was opened successfully a moment ago. Every grant is re-derived, fresh, from a signed proof presented at that exact moment against a DID-keyed trust registry the service itself owns.

Never trust, always verify

No IP allowlist, no long-lived session token, no notion of a "trusted network." Identity and authorization are checked from first principles on every request — the core Zero Trust principle, built on an open DID/VC standard instead of a single vendor's access-control product.

09 — Protecting a Document

Three keys, three jobs — and the top one never touches the file.

Every document gets its own encryption key — a Document Encryption Key, or DEK — the moment it's uploaded. That key used to sit next to the document itself, in the registry and the storage manifest, protected only by being hard to find. It's now wrapped by a master key tied to the document's clearance level, and that master key never leaves the server that issued it.

09 — Protecting a Document

No storage node ever holds a complete, readable document.

The encrypted document lives on Iagon, a decentralized storage network, rather than on one company's server. Every node the network stores it across holds ciphertext only — reassembling it into something a person can actually read happens exclusively after it reaches the wallet that's allowed to decrypt it.

The document service itself holds only a reference — a file ID — to where the network stores the ciphertext, never the data. Its identity (the DID), the index that finds it, and the bytes on disk stay three separate layers, so a storage provider can change, get breached, or shut down without ever touching the document's identity or access rules.

09 — Protecting a Document

Getting the key to the right wallet without ever sending it in the clear.

The server never decrypts the document itself. Unwrapping only recovers the DEK — a fixed 32 bytes — and that's the one thing that needs to reach the wallet. The ciphertext already sitting on Iagon is handed over completely unmodified; it gets decrypted exactly once, locally, after the DEK arrives.

sequenceDiagram
    participant Wallet
    participant Service as Document Service
    Wallet->>Wallet: generate one-time keypair
    Wallet->>Service: send proof + one-time public key
    Service->>Service: unwrap DEK with master key (still just 32 bytes)
    Service->>Service: encrypt DEK to wallet's one-time public key
    Service->>Wallet: encrypted DEK + unmodified ciphertext reference
    Wallet->>Wallet: decrypt DEK, then decrypt document — both locally
Why a DEK at all, then?

The document is already encrypted the moment it's uploaded — that doesn't depend on who eventually opens it. Because that encryption is already done, delivery only ever has to move the DEK, not the document: one fixed-size 32-byte key, re-encrypted per recipient with a single cheap operation, instead of re-encrypting a file that could be megabytes every time someone new is granted access.

Each open generates a brand-new one-time keypair on both sides — a property called perfect forward secrecy. Even if a past session's key were somehow recovered later, it only ever unlocks that one document copy, never anything before or after it.

09 — Protecting a Document

Not every open is a fresh proof — and that's a deliberate trade-off.

The first open of a document runs the full flow: present credentials, verify, decrypt. To avoid asking for that proof again every time the same already-downloaded copy is reopened, the wallet keeps its one-time private key on the device and decrypts locally from then on — no server round-trip.

What this buys
  • Instant repeat access to a copy already opened once
  • Works without a network round-trip
  • No added load on the verification server
What it costs today
  • No server-side record of repeat, cached-key opens
  • The cached key has no expiry of its own
  • Revoking a person's clearance doesn't reach copies they've already cached
Known limitation

This is tracked as a gap, not an oversight — closing it means adding a lightweight, revocation-aware check to cached-key opens without giving up the offline convenience.

10 — What "Verified" Means

Four checks happen before a grant is ever issued.

  • 1
    Signature is genuineThe presentation is cryptographically signed and untampered.
  • 2
    Issuer is trustedThe credential traces back to an issuer this service already recognizes — fail-closed if it doesn't.
  • 3
    Holder-boundThe proof belongs to the DID actually asking right now — not a cached label from an earlier session.
  • 4
    Clearance sufficientThe presented level meets or exceeds what this document requires.
